In their seminal article on the concept of context-aware computing, Schilit et al. \cite{schilit1994context} defined the three basic types of context – user, computing, and physical contexts represented respectively by a user's profile, geographical coordinates and computing environment, and ambient lighting and noise level.

The rapid growth of ubiquitous computing and consumer-grade smart devices has fundamentally transformed the landscape of digital mental health systems, as continuous data streams from smartphones and wearables enable real-time assessment and behavioral inference. At the same time, the anywhere-anytime paradigm facilitates timely, personalized interventions, giving rise to novel mental care strategies, such as Ecological Momentary Assessments (EMA)\cite{stone1994ecological}, Ecological Momentary Interventions (EMI)\cite{balaskas2021ecological}, and more recently, Digital Phenotyping \cite{bufano2023digital} and Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAI)\cite{nahumshani2018jitai}. These strategies complement conventional approaches in mental health care, such as standardized assessments and direct patient-therapist interactions, and generally aim to capture user-relevant data ubiquitously, analyze and process it into actionable insights, and provide real-time, highly granular psychological support. 

In all these strategies, context plays a central, albeit poorly specified, role. Indeed, the very notion of context has been widely discussed by the research community. Dey~\cite{dey2001understanding} defines context as any information that characterizes the situation of an entity, where the entity can be a person, a place, or an object relevant to a user, a system, and their interaction. Coutaz and colleagues~\cite{coutaz2005} recognized the challenges in modeling context in a structured yet flexible manner. The authors distinguished between \textit{context-as-process} and \textit{context-as-state}, the latter reflecting the need for context-aware applications to define and store contextual information in a machine-understandable way. This dichotomy is echoed a decade later by Alegre and colleagues~\cite{alegre2016}, who noted that context-aware applications can be approached from a phenomenological or a positivist perspective. The former, closer to psychology, explains context in complex human behavior and activity as an \textit{interaction problem} (i.e., \textit{context-as-process}), in which context varies depending on the situation, the person, and the surrounding social and environmental circumstances. Empirically, this was supported by an exploratory study on context-aware applications for mental health interventions~\cite{miralles2019}, where participating psychologists cast doubt on which features of context were most relevant to each type of intervention, as this strongly depended on the person and the mental health disorder at hand. The latter, an empirical tradition of positivism that is closer to computer science, traditionally treats context as a \textit{representation problem}, i.e., along the lines of the \textit{context-as-state} view articulated by Coutaz et al.~\cite{coutaz2005}. 

Therefore, we acknowledge the ambiguity of context, and throughout this work, we adhere, as a starting point, to the categorization of Han et al.~\cite{han2008contextaware}, who provided a theoretical framework dividing context into physical (environmental real-world features), internal (user-related internal features), and social (human interaction-based) aspects. In this review, we will further enrich these initial categories through an inductive analysis of the eligible studies. 

Within this context, active data acquisition mechanisms—predominantly notification-delivered questionnaires (i.e., self-reporting) or in-app assessments, such as momentary mood ratings, structured journaling, or active cognitive tasks—have traditionally formed the cornerstone of digital mental health interventions \cite{kraft2024mobile, oudin2023digital}. These active protocols primarily focus on capturing the patient's subjective internal context, encompassing momentary emotional fluctuations, cognitive fatigue, perceived stress, psychiatric symptom severity, and daily lifestyle routines \cite{stone2023evaluation, myingermeys2018esm}. To complement this internal picture, active assessments are also frequently deployed to query external circumstances, such as immediate social settings or engaged activities. However, their reliance on repeated manual user input imposes significant cognitive and interaction burden, which frequently leads to survey fatigue, missing data, and reporting attrition over time \cite{langener2023capturing}.

As a consequence, and driven by the rapid proliferation of modern mobile devices, active self-reporting has increasingly been complemented by passive sensing mechanisms that run unobtrusively in the background \cite{miralles2020smartphone, shen2025passive}. Initially leveraging the built-in sensors of smartphones, passive sensing has progressively expanded to include sensors embedded in consumer wearable devices, such as smartwatches, fitness bands, and smart rings \cite{hassan2025utility, jung2025key}. Passive sensing operates across both internal and external domains, substantially reducing user effort and reporting bias while providing continuous and objective monitoring. On the one hand, sensors embedded in mobile and wearable hardware supplement the user's internal context by tracking physiological and biological correlates of distress, such as heart rate variability, electrodermal activity, and sleep architecture \cite{hickey2021wearablemental, sheikh2021passivesensing}, as well as cognitive load and fatigue through wearable neuroimaging \cite{varandas2022cognitivefatigue}. On the other hand, passive sensing provides unprecedented visibility into the external context that shapes an individual's daily life, capturing physical mobility patterns, ambient environmental parameters, device interaction rhythms, and digital behavioral markers associated with psychological functioning and lifestyle changes \cite{onnela2016digitalphenotyping, binmorshed2021selfesteem, sanudo2020covidactivity}. By continuously modeling these situational factors, passive sensing offers crucial insights into the ecological determinants and situational causes of mental distress. % This includes physical dimensions (e.g., physical and semantic location and noise and ambient light exposure), social dimensions (e.g., social interactions, proximity networks, communication frequencies) and computing/temporal dimensions (e.g., device usage telemetry, temporal behavioral rhythms). 
This notion of digital phenotyping has itself evolved: although it originally referred almost exclusively to using smartphones for data capture~\cite{onnela2016digitalphenotyping}, the concept has progressively expanded~\cite{deboer2023} to account for a wide range of mobile devices as wearable technology has advanced in functional diversity~\cite{ometov2021}. Today, digital phenotyping seeks to quantify the individual human phenotypes (in terms of behavior and physiology) by collecting, processing, and analyzing multi-device and multimodal active and passive data, encompassing both internal and external contextual factors, thereby transforming raw digital signals into actionable digital (bio)markers that serve as indicators of mental health states and symptoms~\cite{bufano2023digital, moura2023digital}.
%Central to the subsequent delivery of personalized, just-in-time adaptive interventions is the integration of internal and external contextual factors to inform and drive therapeutic responses. 

This synthesis of internal and external signals is, in turn, central to the effective delivery of psychological interventions: integrating an individual's internal and external contexts ensures that therapeutic responses are informed, data-driven, and dynamically tailored to evolving psychological needs and ecological circumstances. Beyond improving the identification and quantification of potential causal triggers, external contextual information enables interventions to be dynamically adapted based on the individual's ecological context, such as location (e.g., absolute, relative location, proximity), temporal factors (e.g., time of day, weekday/weekend, holiday), activity/behavioral context (i.e., engaged activities, daily routines), social circumstances (e.g., being alone, social interactions), situational factors(e.g., at home, at work, in a public place), or environmental conditions(e.g., weather, noise levels)~\cite{bufano2023digital, onnela2016digitalphenotyping}. 

Despite this potential and emerging integrative research, there remains a divide between digital phenotyping, which primarily focuses on uncovering digital markers for monitoring and prediction~\cite{choi2024digital}, and the integration of passive sensing and the resulting markers into personalized, adaptive mental health interventions~\cite{bosch2025integration, choi2024digital}. State-of-the-art JITAI systems still rely primarily on self-reported data; yet with a growing body of literature reporting passive sensing data~\cite{hsu2025personalized, somkuwar2026transforming}, advanced concepts, such as (derived) individuals’ states and external contexts, are hardly used~\cite{van2025beyond}.

Current literature continues to focus primarily on downstream diagnostic prediction, longitudinal symptom tracking, or limited device ecosystems. For example, Lee et al. (2023) concentrate on digital phenotyping to detect and predict general patterns of behavior, while Busshart et al. Moreover, even when the clinical scope of the reviews is broad, the focus is often overwhelmingly on observation rather than treatment. For example, Dumas et al. (2026) include both psychiatric and non-psychiatric physical or neurological conditions (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, sleep apnea), but the intervention focus is absent, with intervention delivery present only in a minuscule fraction (3\%, 2 out of 65 studies). We make a distinctive contribution by limiting our scope to digital mental health interventions that provide active real-time support, as opposed to these paradigms that are high on tracking and exclusive to phenotyping. Moreover, our eligibility criteria specifically restrict the target population to those with recognized mental health disorders based on DSM-5 guidelines, unlike previous studies that mostly used non-clinical samples or pooled unrelated physical diseases. Instead, we systematically review and classify how a broad hardware ecosystem, including smartphones and consumer-grade wearables, is used to capture a rich, multimodal representation of physical, social,l and computing/temporal environments to inform the triggering and tailoring of Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs) and Ecological Momentary Interventions (EMIs), instead of restricting the use of internal physiological signals or single smartphone sensor configurations.

%\subsection{Objectives}
This systematic review aims to categorize and analyze the use of external contextual factors in digital interventions for recognized mental health disorders, delivered through consumer smart devices and wearable technologies. Specifically, this review maps which external context information is captured and how it is leveraged to define context- and situation-aware intervention models. Furthermore, we assess the maturity and level of evaluation across the body of research analyzed, ultimately identifying current gaps and future research opportunities. %We furthermore aim to identify technological capabilities and gaps. %\cite{adler2024actionablesensing}.
Specifically, this review addresses the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=Q\arabic*, itemsep=0pt]   
    \item Which mental health disorders are targeted by context-aware interventions?
    \item What hardware platforms were used to implement these interventions?
    \item What types of external contextual data are captured for these interventions?
    \item What sensing technologies and methods are used to collect external context data?
    \item How is external context data used (purpose) in interventions? 
    \item What context delivery mechanisms are employed in interventions?
    \item What evaluation designs and dimensions are used to assess the interventions?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Rationale}

Context-aware computing is an idea that was first introduced by Schilit et al. \cite{schilit1994context} back in 1994, defining it as a type of software that can dynamically adjust itself to its spatial env ironment, the surrounding individuals and objects, as well as any changes occurring within the temporal environment. The next step towards developing a better understanding of this concept was made by Dey \cite{dey2001understanding}, who introduced an alternative definition based on task-oriented considerations and described context awareness as using information and offering services relevant to the particular tasks of a person. Today, context awareness has moved from being a mere topic to becoming a field of study; moreover, it has become one of the core areas of ubiquitous computing.

There are many approaches to the classification of contextual data used in the literature, depending mainly on the purposes of analysis or theoretical approaches involved in the investigation of the issue \cite{mostefaoui2004contextaware}. In their seminal article on the concept of context-aware computing, Schilit et al. \cite{schilit1994context} defined the three basic types of context – user, computing, and physical contexts represented respectively by a user's profile, geographical coordinates, and computing environment, and ambient lighting and noise level.

Focusing on context parameters that are especially important for mobile applications, some early research \cite{schilit2000customizingmobile} suggested considering four major categories of context – location, identity, time, and activity as the most basic metrics that make it possible to conclude more specific contextual factors. Thus, identifying the identity of a person allows inferring his preferences, contacts, and relations with others, and the identification of his location allows analyzing potential activities in a place and the presence of other persons or objects around him.

Context may also be classified on the basis of user's subjective perceptions and sensations. In this case, Gwizdka \cite{gwizdka2000context} distinguished internal and external context. The former implies the identification of mental and emotional states of a person, and includes such factors as task state, personal events, and communication logs, while the latter relates to the conditions of the outer world and involves the location of a person, time, and proximity to other people and devices.

In terms of HCI theory within location-based systems, Petrelli et al. \cite{petrelli2000modeling} distinguished material and social context. Material context refers to the physical space and existing technological resources (type of a device used, network availability, or GPS), and social context relates to personal characteristics of a user, his interest, and the current context, such as whether he is alone or with somebody else.

The rapid growth of ubiquitous computing and consumer-grade smart devices has fundamentally transformed the landscape of psychiatric monitoring and psychological support. The leverage of continuous data streams provided by smartphones and wearable technology has successfully revolutionized digital health systems such as Ecological Momentary Assessments (EMA)\cite{fortuna2022peersupportedema}, Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAI)\cite{vangenugten2025jitai}, and Ecological Momentary Interventions (EMI)\cite{balaskas2021ecological}. These methodologies give a real-time, highly granular behavioral support beyond the traditional clinical settings. However, the paradigm in this domain has predominantly focused on passive sensing of internal context\cite{nelson2018smarthome}, specifically internal physiological metrics such as heart rate variability (HRV), electrodermal activity (EDA), and sleep architecture\cite{sheikh2021passivesensing}.

While tracking internal physiological and biological metrics is highly effective for detecting the presence of physiological stimulus or the manifestation of psychiatric symptoms\cite{lanata2015complexity}, it suffers from a major critical diagnostic limitation: it identifies that distress is occurring without understanding why it is occurring. Relying exclusively on internal symptom tracking creates a fundamental blind spot regarding the situational and environmental triggers of mental health disorders. Consequently, there is a distinct "why" gap in current frameworks for ubiquitous computing for mental health.

To bridge this gap, intervention architectures must shift toward the capture and analysis of external context. External context sensing focuses on the situational causes of distress by continuously modeling the user's environment. This includes physical dimensions ( Physical and semantic location, and noise and ambient light exposure), social dimensions (Social interactions, proximity networks, and communication frequencies), and computing/temporal dimensions (such as device usage telemetry and temporal behavioral rhythms). The integration of multidimensional context variables enables algorithmic systems to map the events that lead to mental health distresses. Transitioning from passive symptom sensing to active, situation-aware monitoring gives a solid foundation for developing symptom-reactive treatments and interventions\cite{wang2024contextaware}.

Although this shift is theoretically necessary and beneficial, the extent to which external context variables are effectively extracted, integrated, and utilized within current consumer-grade digital health interventions remains fragmented. A structured evaluation of the literature is required to determine how context-aware computing maps external environmental factors to mental health outcomes.

\subsection{Objectives}
This systematic review aims to categorize and analyze external contextual factors and how they affect the mental health interventions delivered through wearable technologies and smart devices. By this evaluation of the process of integrating these context variables, this review seeks to systematically map the transition from internal physiological tracking to a more robust, situation-aware intervention model, identifying the technological capabilities and gaps.

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Background:}
Ubiquitous computing underpins Ecological Momentary Interventions (EMIs) and Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs) for mental health. However, this paradigm emphasizes internal physiological sensing (symptom tracking) over external contextual factors (situational causes), even though these factors are directly correlated with the mental issue.\newline

\noindent
\textbf{Objectives:}
This study systematically reviews, categorizes, and analyzes how physical, social, and computing/temporal contexts are integrated into consumer wearable and smartphone-based mental health interventions.\newline

\noindent
\textbf{Eligibility Criteria:}
Peer-reviewed articles from journals and conferences that evaluated mental health interventions utilizing consumer-grade wearables or smartphones, with a primary focus on adaptive external context sensing, were included. Studies that used clinical-grade diagnostics or exclusively tracked internal physiological metrics without external environmental correlation were excluded.\newline

\noindent
\textbf{Sources:}
We conducted a comprehensive search across Scopus, PsycINFO, PubMed, and Web of Science.\newline

\noindent
\textbf{Selection Methods:}
Following PRISMA guidelines, titles and abstracts were screened using automated machine learning tools(ASReview), followed by independent full-text reviews. Data were extracted utilizing a standardized Physical, Social, and Computing framework.\newline

\textbf{Conclusions:}
The shift from passive symptom monitoring to active, situation-aware intervention models shows an enhanced ecological validity of digital mental health tools. Bridging the existing "why" gap in ubiquitous computing, upcoming studies must consider prioritizing multimodal external sensing while targeting robust, privacy-preserving computational systems.\newline

\end{abstract}

\section{Methods}

This systematic review is reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines~\cite{page2021prisma}. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_prisma}.  

% PRISMA Item 5
\subsection{Eligibility Criteria}

To ensure the included studies were relevant to the goal of this systematic review, we defined explicit inclusion criteria (IC). These criteria identify studies describing digital mental health interventions that (i) address a mental disorder, (ii) primarily rely on consumer-grade mobile devices, and (iii) collect and use external contextual information. A study was eligible only if all of the following criteria were met:


\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IC1 (Targeted Disorders):} The study addressed individuals affected by a recognized mental health disorder, as identified in the DSM-5~\cite{APA2022DSM5TR}, who were either diagnosed or screened as having elevated symptoms, and the authors explicitly framed the intervention as targeting that disorder and/or its symptoms.

    \item \textbf{IC2 (Mobile Mental Health Intervention)}: The study described a mental health intervention delivered through a mobile app.
    
    \item \textbf{IC3 (External contextualization):} The intervention used consumer-grade mobile devices and technologies (e.g., smartphones, smartwatches, fitness trackers, smart rings, or other mobile devices capable of sensing external contextual events) to collect external contextual data that could be used by the intervention.

    \item \textbf{IC4 (Type of Publication):} The report describing the study was a full, peer-reviewed paper published in a journal or conference proceedings and written in English.
\end{itemize}

Accordingly, the following were excluded: studies addressing physical health, psychological symptoms, or well-being outside the context of a recognized mental disorder (IC1); reported interventions delivered through non-mobile channels and technologies, such as face-to-face therapies or internet- and web-based interventions (IC2); studies that did not use contextual data, used only internal contextual data (e.g., physiological signals), relied on specialized (non-consumer) devices, or used contextual data solely for assessment (i.e., digital phenotyping) without integrating it into an intervention (IC3); and gray literature, secondary research studies, workshop papers, and other non-peer-reviewed or non-full-length reports (IC4).

\subsection{Information Sources}   

Four bibliographic databases were searched: Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate), APA PsycInfo, and PubMed (MEDLINE, National Library of Medicine). 
Searches were conducted across all databases, with no time restrictions applied, up to February 5, 2026, the date on which the final search was performed in each of the sources.

% PRISMA item 7
\subsection{Search and Study Selection}

Because the review question spans clinical, technological, and contextual concepts, the search strategy was not structured using the PICOC framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Context)~\cite{carrerarivera2022pico}. Instead, it combined (Boolean operator AND) four conceptual blocks, each formed by the disjunction (OR) of synonyms and related terms: (i) mental health disorders (e.g., mental disorder, depression, anxiety); (ii) consumer mobile technologies and sensing paradigms (e.g., wearables, smartphones, ubiquitous computing, passive sensing); (iii) external context and environmental factors (e.g., physical location, ambient light, social proximity, device usage); and (iv) intervention delivery mechanisms (e.g., Ecological Momentary Interventions [EMI], Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions [JITAI], therapy). 

Keywords within each block were deliberately broad rather than restrictive, aiming for the largest possible initial set of potentially relevant studies. The initial set of terms was extracted from known relevant studies and recent reviews, and was subsequently refined through interactions with the databases.

In line with the eligibility criteria (IC4), the search was explicitly restricted to English-language journal articles and conference papers within relevant subject areas (i.e., Medicine, Computer Science, Psychology, and Social Sciences). The Scopus query was designed first and then translated manually into semantically equivalent queries adapted to the syntax and field tags of each selected database.
%All database-specific queries were semantically equivalent but formulated using the syntax and technical support of the respective search engines. 
The full search strings, exactly as run in each database (Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, and APA PsycInfo), including all filters and limits, are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_search_strings}.

\subsection{Selection Process}   

Records retrieved from all databases were merged, and duplicates were removed automatically in Zotero, followed by manual checking. Title, abstract, and keyword screening was performed by the first author, assisted by an open-source, AI-assisted tool, ASReview LAB v3.0rc0
\footnote{\url{https://asreview.nl/}} \cite{vandeschoot2021asreview}. This tool uses active learning to prioritize records: after each screening decision, the model re-ranked the unscreened records so that those most likely to be relevant were presented first. \tbc{Model configuration, e.g., feature extractor, classifier, query strategy; and the prior knowledge used to start the model, i.e., number of relevant and irrelevant records and how they were chosen}.ASReview was used only to prioritize records; inclusion and exclusion decisions were made by the first author. Records judged eligible, or whose eligibility could not be determined from the title and abstract, were retrieved in full text for thorough assessment. Each full-text report was assessed by two reviewers against the eligibility criteria. Each reviewer worked independently and without access to each other's decisions. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer in case of conflict. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded and are reported in the PRISMA flow diagram (See section~\ref{sec:results}). Study authors were not contacted to confirm eligibility.

\subsection{Data Collection Process}

The variables to be extracted were defined in a brainstorming session involving all authors. Before full extraction, the extraction form was piloted on five articles to refine the variables and the extraction procedure. After the pilot, the first author extracted data from eligible studies using a structured form (a shared spreadsheet). Data were extracted manually from the published studies only. The extraction form is available in the code/data repository in Section~\ref{sec:methods:dasa}.

\subsection{Data Items}
\label{sec:methods:data-items}

In addition to bibliographic information, each included study was coded along seven dimensions that correspond with the research questions posed in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}: targeted mental disorders, technological platforms, external context types (i.e., \textit{what?}), external context-capturing techniques (i.e., \textit{how?}), external context purpose in interventions (i.e., \textit{what for?}), intervention delivery mechanisms, and evaluation designs and dimensions (\textit{type and target}). Subcategory schemes were either drawn from existing literature or derived inductively from the included studies, as detailed below. Note that each study could be assigned to more than one subcategory within a given dimension. If information was missing, the item was coded as ``NA''.

\textbf{Targeted mental disorders (Q1)}. The mental disorder(s) targeted by each intervention were coded according to the DSM-5~\cite{APA2022DSM5TR}. Participant diagnostic status was also categorized into five levels: (1) disorder specifically stated and participants clinically diagnosed; (2) disorder stated, but participants identified only through a screening instrument; (3) disorder not explicitly named (e.g., ``depressive symptoms'' or ``elevated symptoms''), with participants identified through screening; (4) disorder stated or used to frame the study, but participants were neither clinically diagnosed nor screened; and (5) disorder not specifically stated, with participants screened for possible symptoms.

% 1/ DISORDER DIAGNOSED - GREEN: authors state: patients with depression, and patients are DIAGNOSED
% 2/ DISORDER STATED, NOT DIAGNOSED, SCREENED - CYAN: authors state: patients with depression, but they only apply a SCREENING test
% 3/ DISORDER NOT STATED, NOT DIAGNOSED, SCREENED - RED: authors state: patient with depressive symptoms or "heightened symptoms"; they only apply a SCREENING test
% 4/ STATED or FRAMED, NOT DIAGNOSED NOT SCREENED - PINK: authors state: patient with depression or in the context of depression or depressive symptoms, but not diagnosed or screened.
% 5/ DISORDER NOTE STATED, but SCREENED - YELLOW: the disorder is not specifically stated, but they screen for participants with (possible) symptoms


\textbf{Technological platform (Q2)}. The consumer-grade mobile technologies were inductively extracted from the eligible studies and grouped into mobile phones (smartphones), wrist-worn wearables (e.g., smartwatches and wristbands), tablets, and Positioning sensors (e.g., Bluetooth Low Energy [BLE] beacons). 

\textbf{External context type (Q3)}. Context types were adapted from the theoretical framework of Han et al. \cite{han2008contextaware}, which distinguishes physical (features of the real-world environment), internal (user-related features), and social (interaction-based) context. Given that internal context falls outside the scope of the review (IC3), the physical and social contexts were expanded to include a third main category: computing and temporal context. Subcategories relevant to these three main categories were derived inductively from the included studies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Physical Context (metrics referring to the user's geospatial position, movement, and physical environment): absolute geolocation (e.g., coordinates, outdoor location); ambient environment (e.g., noise, light, seasons); mobility and movement (e.g., distance traveled, location variance, mobility patterns); semantic context (e.g., home vs. away, clinic, rural/urban, specific events);
    
    \item Social Context (metrics referring to the user's social engagement, proximity network, or level of socialization): clinical/coach support (e.g., contact with the research team, clinician or coach communication, adherence support); communication (e.g., digital communication patterns, text or speech volume, network size); peer/community interaction (e.g., peer influence, group forums, perceived social support, community interactions); and physical proximity (e.g., ambient human presence, in-person socialization, physical proximity to others).
    
    \item Computing and Temporal Context (metrics related to the user's device usage, temporal rhythms, or time-related information): in-app telemetry (e.g., logins, session duration and frequency, screen views, clicks);
    macro-temporal events (e.g., longitudinal measurements, total active days, weeks of use, streaks); 
    micro-temporal events (e.g., timestamps, time of day, latency, circadian rhythms); and task completion (e.g., modules finished, therapy activities, sessions attended, assignments). 
\end{itemize}    
    
\textbf{External context-capturing techniques (Q4)}. Eleven techniques used to capture external context were observed in the corpus: GPS/geofencing; Wi-Fi network logging; motion/accelerometer data; microphone/audio recordings; Bluetooth sensors; light sensors; in-app tracking and telemetry (e.g., in-app forums, usage metrics); online usage tracking (e.g., server or cloud logs, web analytics); device-side automation (e.g., background usage tracking, metadata); self-report (e.g., ecological momentary assessment, surveys); and direct contact (e.g., SMS, automated emails).

\textbf{External context purpose in interventions (Q5)}. We coded how external context information was used within the intervention from a psychology perspective: adaptive therapy (e.g., personalized feedback, tailored notifications); healthcare provider support (e.g., clinician dashboard, care monitoring); learning algorithm (e.g., risk prediction, personalization models); and user support (e.g., psychoeducation, self-management tools). 

\textbf{Intervention delivery mechanisms (Q6)} Closely related to the previous categorization (intervention purpose), we coded delivery context mechanisms as trigger-based, scheduled, algorithmic, dashboard-mediated, or user-initiated. These specific mechanisms were, in turn, grouped into three delivery paradigms: \textit{active interventions}, which require direct, conscious engagement or decision-making from the user or clinician within the application; \textit{passive interventions}, which operate autonomously in the background without requiring user initiative; and \textit{hybrid interventions}, which pair automated background processes with human decision-making or collaborative workflows.

\textbf{Evaluation designs and dimensions (Q7)}. We coded both the evaluation target, i.e., the outcome domains assessed (i.e., clinical efficacy and effectiveness, usability and user experience, user engagement and adherence, acceptability and feasibility), and the evaluation design (i.e., randomized controlled trial, pilot and feasibility studies, observational and quasi-experimental studies, formative and design studies). Outcome domains were recorded as reported by the study authors. Individual outcome measures and effect estimates were not extracted since the review did not aim to synthesize intervention effects.

\subsection{Study Risk of Bias Assessment, Measures, and Certainty Assessment}   
 
Because this review aimed to describe how external context is acquired and used in mobile mental health interventions, rather than to estimate their effects, and because the included studies were highly heterogeneous in design (ranging from design studies to randomized controlled trials), no formal assessment of the risk of bias in individual studies was performed. For the same reason, no effect measures were computed, and no quantitative synthesis of intervention effects was performed. Consequently, neither the risk of bias due to missing results nor the certainty of the body of evidence was assessed. The implications of these choices are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Synthesis Methods}   

All included studies were eligible for the synthesis. The synthesis was descriptive, as studies were tabulated according to the coding scheme by dimension described in Section~\ref{sec:methods:data-items}. Results are presented in a summary table listing the coding of each included study (Table~\ref{tab:context_studies}) and in Figures~\ref{fig:bubble_matrix}, \ref{fig:intervention_mechanisms_stacked}, and \ref{fig:metrics_designs_heatmap} that show the distribution and co-occurrence of categories, complemented by a narrative summary for each dimension. The analysis for the generation of figures was performed in Python 3.13.16; the corresponding code and data are available in the repository indicated in Section~\ref{sec:methods:dasa}.

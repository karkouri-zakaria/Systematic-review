\section{Methods}

This systematic review is reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines~\cite{page2021prisma}. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Appendix~\tbc{X}. 

% PRISMA Item 5
\subsection{Eligibility Criteria}

To ensure the included studies were relevant to this systematic review's goals, we defined explicit inclusion criteria (IC). These criteria identify studies describing digital mental health interventions that (i) address a mental disorder, (ii) primarily rely on consumer-grade mobile devices, and (iii) collect and use external contextual information. A study was eligible only if all of the following criteria were met:


\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IC1 (Targeted Disorders):} the study addressed individuals affected by a recognized mental health disorder, as identified in the DSM-5~\cite{APA2022DSM5TR}, who were either diagnosed or screened as having elevated symptoms, and the authors explicitly framed the intervention as targeting that disorder and/or its symptoms.

    \item \textbf{IC2 (Mobile Mental Health Intervention)}: The study described a mental health intervention delivered through a mobile app.
    
    \item \textbf{IC3 (External contextualization):} The intervention used consumer-grade mobile devices and technologies (e.g., smartphones, smartwatches, fitness trackers, smart rings, or other mobile device capable of sensing external contextual) to collect external contextual data to be used by the intervention.

    \item \textbf{IC4 (Type of Publication):} The report describing the study was a full, peer-reviewed paper published in a journal or conference proceedings and written in English.
\end{itemize}

Accordingly, the following were excluded: studies addressing physical health, psychological symptoms, or well-being outside the context of a recognized mental disorder (IC1); reported interventions delivered through non-mobile channels and technologies, such as face-to-face therapies or internet- and web-based interventions (IC2); studies that did not use contextual data, used only internal contextual data (e.g., physiological signals), relied on specialized (non-consumer) devices, or used contextual data solely for assessment (i.e., digital phenotyping) without integrating it into an intervention (IC3); and gray literature, secondary research studies, workshop papers, and other non-peer-reviewed or non-full-length reports (IC4).

\subsection{Information Sources}   

Four bibliographic databases were searched: Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate), APA PsycInfo, and PubMed (MEDLINE, National Library of Medicine). 
Searches were conducted across all databases, with no time restrictions applied, up to February 5, 2026, the date on which the final search was performed in each of the sources.

% PRISMA item 7
\subsection{Search and Study Selection}

Because the review question spans clinical, technological, and contextual concepts, the search strategy was not structured using the PICOC framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Context)~\cite{carrerarivera2022pico}. Instead, it combined, by Boolean conjunction (AND), four conceptual blocks, each formed by the disjunction (OR) of synonyms and related terms: (i) mental health disorders (e.g., mental disorder, depression, anxiety); (ii) consumer mobile technologies and sensing paradigms (e.g., wearable, smartphones, ubiquitous computing, passive sensing); (iii) external context and environmental factors (e.g., physical location, ambient light, social proximity, device usage); and (iv) intervention delivery mechanisms (e.g., Ecological Momentary Interventions [EMI], Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions [JITAI], therapy). 

Keywords within each block were deliberately broad rather than restrictive, aiming at the largest possible initial set of potentially relevant studies. The initial set of terms was extracted from known relevant studies and recent reviews, and was subsequently refined through interactions with the databases.

In line with the eligibility criteria (IC4), the search was explicitly restricted to English-language journal articles and conference papers within relevant subject areas (i.e., Medicine, Computer Science, Psychology, and Social Sciences). The Scopus query was designed first and then translated manually into semantically equivalent queries adapted to the syntax and field tags of each selected database.

\subsection{Selection Process}   

Records retrieved from all databases were merged, and duplicates were removed \tbc{tool and method, e.g., automatically in Zotero followed by manual checking}. Title, abstract, and keyword screening was performed by the first author assisted by an open-source, AI-assisted tool -- ASReview LAB \tbc{version} \footnote{\url{https://asreview.nl/}} \cite{vandeschoot2021asreview}. This tool uses active learning to prioritize records: after each screening decision, the model re-ranked the unscreened records so that those most likely to be relevant were presented first. \tbc{Model configuration, e.g., feature extractor, classifier, query strategy; and the prior knowledge used to start the model, i.e., number of relevant and irrelevant records and how they were chosen.} ASReview was used only to prioritize records; inclusion and exclusion decisions were made by the first author. Records judged eligible, or whose eligibility could not be determined from the title and abstract, were retrieved in full text for a through assessment. Each full-text report was assessed by two reviewers against the eligibility criteria. Each reviewer worked independently and without access to each other's decisions. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer in case of conflict. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded and are reported in the PRISMA flow diagram (See section~\ref{sec:results}). Study authors were not contacted to confirm eligibility.

\subsection{Data Items}
\label{sec:methods:data-items}

In addition to bibliographic information, each included study was coded along six dimensions that correspond with the research questions posed in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}: targeted mental disorders, technological platforms, external context types (i.e., \textit{what?}), external context-capturing techniques (i.e., \textit{how?}), intervention integration (i.e., \textit{what for?}), and study evaluation (\textit{type and target}). Category schemes were either taken from existing literature or derived inductively from the included studies, as described below.

\textbf{Targeted mental disorders}. The disorder targeted by each intervention was coded according to the DSM-5~\cite{APA2022DSM5TR} categories. The diagnostic status of participants was also coded in five levels: (1) disorder stated and participants clinically diagnosed; (2) disorder stated, but participants identified only through a screening instrument; (3) disorder not explicitly named (e.g., ``depressive symptoms'' or ``elevated symptoms''), with participants identified through screening; (4) disorder stated or used to frame the study, but participants neither diagnosed nor screened; and (5) disorder not specifically stated, with participants screened for possible symptoms.

% 3/ DISORDER NOT STATED, NOT DIAGNOSED, SCREENED - RED: authors state: patient with depressive symptoms or "heightened symptoms", they only apply SCREENING test
% 4/ STATED or FRAMED, NOT DIAGNOSED NOT SCREENED - PINK: authors state: patient with depression or in the context of depression or depressive symptoms, but not diagnosed not screened.

\textbf{Technological platform}. The consumer-grade mobile technologies were inductively extracted from the eligible studies and grouped into mobile phones (smartphones), wrist-worn wearables (e.g., smartwatches and wristbands), tablets, and specialized sensors (e.g., Bluetooth Low Energy [BLE] beacons). 

\textbf{External context type}. Context types were adapted from the theoretical framework of Han et al. \cite{han2008contextaware}, which distinguishes physical (features of the real-world environment), internal (user-related features), and social (interaction-based) context. Given that the internal context fell outside the scope of the review (IC3), the subcategories relevant to the physical and social contexts were derived inductively from the included studies, resulting in three categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Physical Context (metrics referring to the user's geospatial position, movement and physical environment): absolute geolocation (e.g., coordinates, outdoor location); activities and routines (e.g., meals, sleep proxies, activity levels); ambient environment (e.g., noise, light, seasons); mobility and movement (e.g., distance traveled, location variance, mobility patterns); semantic context (e.g., home vs. away, clinic, rural/urban, specific events), virtual and simulated environment (e.g., virtual reality).
    
    \item Social Context (metrics referring to the user's social engagement, proximity network or level of socialization): clinical/coach support (e.g., contact with research team, clinician or coach communication, adherence support); communication (e.g., digital communication patterns, text or speech volume, network size); peer/community interaction (e.g., peer influence, group forums, perceived social support, community interactions); and physical proximity (e.g., ambient human presence, in-person socialization, physical proximity to others).
    
    \item Computing and Temporal Context (metrics related to the user's device usage, temporal rhythms, or time-related information): in-app telemetry (e.g., logins, session duration and frequency, screen views, clicks); %macro-timeline
    macro-temporal patterns (e.g., longitudinal measurements, total active days, weeks of use, streaks); %micro-temporal 
    micro-temporal patterns (e.g., timestamps, time of day, latency, circadian rhythms); and task completion (e.g., modules finished, therapy activities, sessions attended, assignments). 
\end{itemize}    
    
\textbf{External context-capturing techniques}. The techniques used to capture external context were derived inductively: GPS/geofencing; Wi-Fi network logging; motion/accelerometer data; microphone/audio recordings; Bluetooth sensing; light sensors; VR/AR headsets; in-app tracking and telemetry (e.g., in-app forums, usage metrics); online usage tracking (e.g., server or cloud logs, web analytics); device-side automation (e.g., background usage tracking, metadata), self-report (e.g., ecology momentary assessment, surveys), and direct contact and prompts (e.g., SMS, automated emails). 

\textbf{Intervention integration}. We coded how external context information was used within the intervention from an psychology perspective: adaptive therapy (e.g., personalized feedback, tailored notifications); healthcare provider support (e.g., clinician dashboard, care monitoring); learning algorithm (e.g., risk prediction, personalization models), and user support (e.g., psychoeducation, self-management tools). Apart from the psychology-oriented categorization, we alternatively coded the use of context in interventions based on delivery mechamisms and paradigms. Here, we extracted differente  delivery mechanisms (trigger-based, manual. algrotome, dasnborads, scalened user-driven) across three delivery paradigms: active, passive, and hybrid interventions.

\textbf{Study evaluation}. We coded both the evaluation target, i.e. the outcome domains assessed (i.e., clinical efficacy and effectiveness, usability and user experience, user engagement and adherence, acceptability and feasibility) and the evaluation design (i.e., randomized controlled trial, pilot and feasibility studies, observational and quasi-experimental studies, formative and design studies). \subsection{Study Risk of Bias Assessment, Measures, and Certainty Assessment}   
 
Because this review aimed to describe how external context is acquired and used in mobile mental health interventions, rather than to estimate their effects, and because the included studies were highly heterogeneous in design (from design studies to randomized controlled trials), no formal assessment of risk of bias (PRISMA item 11, see \cite{page2021prisma}) in individual studies was performed. Similarly, no effect measures (PRISMA item 12) were computed or synthesized, as the review did not aim to quantify intervention effects, and no quantitative synthesis of intervention effects was performed; neither the risk of bias due to missing results (PRISMA item 14) nor the certainty of the body of evidence (PRISMA item 15) was assessed. \todo{CARLOS to check out "Limitations of the Evidence" and "Limitations of the Review Process" to verify if there are methods to check risk of bias due to missing results (PRISMA item 14) and the certainty of the body of evidence (PRISMA item 15)}.

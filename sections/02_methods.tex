\section{Methods}

\subsection{Search and Study Selection}
This systematic review uses the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We performed an extensive search of scientific databases, namely Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), PsycINFO, and PubMed, using complex queries constructed with Boolean operators. The search strategy was designed to intersect (logical conjunction) four core dimensions: mental health disorders (e.g., mental disorder, depression, anxiety), consumer mobile technologies and sensing paradigms (e.g., wearable, smartphones, ubiquitous computing, passive sensing), external context and environmental factors (e.g., physical location, ambient light, social proximity, device usage), and intervention delivery mechanisms (e.g., Ecological Momentary Interventions [EMI], Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions [JITAI], therapy). Keywords were comprehensive rather than restrictive, aiming at the largest possible initial set of potentially relevant studies. An example The search was explicitly restricted to English-language journal articles and conference papers within relevant subject areas (i.e., Medicine, Computer Science, Psychology, and Social Sciences). All database-specific queries were semantically equivalent but formulated using the different syntaxes and technical support of the respective search engines. An example query for Scopus can be found in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_scopus}. The initial search was performed on the 5th of February 2026, without applying any publication date restrictions. All resulting publications were downloaded, and duplicates were removed. We conducted the initial screening of titles, abstracts, and keywords against the inclusion (see Section \ref{Inclusion Criteria (IC)}) using ASReview\footnote{\url{https://asreview.nl/}} \cite{vandeschoot2021asreview}, an open-source, AI-assisted screening tool developed by Utrecht University. All articles were screen by two authors, with the third acting as tie-breaking referee in case of conflict. Subsequently, papers that remained inconclusive were fully reviewed according to the same procedure to check their eligibility using the IC.

\subsection{Eligibility Criteria}

To guarantee a collection of scientific articles related to the goals of the current systematic review, clear inclusion criteria (IC) were defined. These criteria were designed to identify studies that describe digital mental health interventions to address a mental disorder, that use consumer-grade mobile devices and acquire and exploit external contextual information.

\subsubsection*{Inclusion Criteria (IC)}
\label{Inclusion Criteria (IC)}

An article was considered eligible if the following requirements were met:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IC1 (Targeted Disorders):} The study addressed individuals affected by a recognized mental health disorder (as identified in DSM-5 \cite{APA2022DSM5TR}), or with elevated symptoms in the context of a DSM-5 disorder, and provides a digital mental health intervention for that disorder and/or its symptoms, and the authors identify it as such.
    %The research addresses individuals affected by mental health conditions (depression, anxiety, PTSD, bipolar disorder, etc.) or investigates interventions aiming to improve psychological well-being.
    
    \item \textbf{IC2 (Technological Platforms):} The study leverages consumer-grade mobile devices and technologies, such as smartphones, smartwatches, fitness trackers, smart rings, or any other of devices suitable for sensing and/or intervention delivery.

    \item \textbf{IC3 (Mobile Mental Health Intervention): The study describes a mental health intervention delivered using an app on a mobile device.}
    
    \item \textbf{IC4 (External contextualization):} The study captures and uses external contextual information in the digital mental health intervention.
    
    \item \textbf{IC5 (Type of Publication):} We consider full research articles, which are peer-reviewed, published in a journal or conference proceedings and written in English.
\end{itemize}

Following these inclusion criteria, this study explicitly excludes studies generically addressing physical health, psychological symptoms or well-being, outside the context of a recognized mental disorder (IC1); studies using traditional (none mobile or wearable) technologies, such as traditional therapies, internet and web-based interventions (IC2); studies only using internal context data (IC4), or focused solely on assessment (i.e., digital phenotyping) without integrating their use in an intervention (IC3); studies published in grey literature, secondary research studies (IC5).

\subsection{Classification of Studies}
The studies were classified along six dimensions: targeted mental disorders, technological platforms (i.e., \textit{which mobile and/or wearable technology is used?}), external context types (i.e., \textit{what?}), external context capturing techniques (i.e., \textit{how?}), intervention integration (i.e., \textit{what for?}), and study evaluation (\textit{type and target}). For the targeted mental disorders, those listed in the DSM-5 \cite{APA2022DSM5TR} were considered. For the technological platforms, any consumer-grade mobile technology was inductively extracted from the dataset, including mobile phones (smartphones), wrist-worn wearables (e.g., smartwatches and wristbands), tablets, and specialized sensors (e.g., Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) beacons). External context types were adapted from the theoretical framework of Han et al. \cite{han2008contextaware}, which divides context into physical (environmental real-world features), internal (internal user-related features), and social (human interaction-based) aspects. Further subcategorization was inductively derived from the dataset. Consistent with the review's focus on external context factors, we thus distinguish Physical Context (any metric referring to the user's geospatial position, movement and physical environment), with subcategories absolute geolocation (e.g., coordinates, outdoor location, exact user location), activities and routines (e.g., meals, sleep proxy, activity levels), ambient environment (e.g., noise, light, seasons), mobility and movement (e.g., distance, variance, patterns), semantic context (e.g., home vs. away, clinic, rural/urban, specific events), virtual and simulated (e.g., VR environments); Social Context (any metric referring to the user's social engagement, proximity network or level of socialization), with subcategories in-app telemetry (e.g., logins, duration, frequency, screen/page views, clicks), macro-timeline (e.g., longitudinal measurements, total active days, weeks of use, streaks), micro-temporal (e.g., exact timestamps, specific time of day, latency, circadian rhythms), task completion (e.g., modules finished, therapy activities, sessions attended, assignments); and Computing and Temporal Context (any metric related to the user's device(s) usage, their temporal rhythms or time-related information), with subcategories clinical/coach support (e.g., research team support, direct coach/clinician communication, adherence interventions), communication (e.g., digital patterns, text/speech volume, network size), peer/community (e.g., peer influence, group forums, perceived social support, community interactions), physical proximity (e.g., ambient human presence, in-person socialization, physical proximity to others). For external context capturing techniques, we inductively summarize how external context information is captured: GPS / Geofencing, Wi-Fi network logging, Motion / Accelerometer data, Microphone / Audio recordings, Bluetooth sensors, Light Sensors, VR / AR Headsets, In-App Tracking \& Telemetry (e.g., in-app forums, usage metrics), Online Usage tracking (e.g., server/cloud logs, Google Analytics), Device-side Automation (e.g., background usage tracking, metadata), Self-Report (e.g., EMAs, surveys), Direct Contact \& Prompts (e.g., SMS, automated emails). For intervention integration, we categorized how external context information is exploited in the intervention: adaptive therapy (e.g., personalized feedback, tailored notifications), healthcare manager support (e.g., clinician dashboard, care monitoring), learning algorithm (e.g., risk prediction, personalization models), user support (e.g., psychoeducation, self-management tools). For the study evaluation, we considered both what was evaluated (i.e., clinical efficacy and effectiveness, usability and user experience, user engagement and adherence, acceptability and feasibility) and the type of evaluation (i.e., randomized controlled trial, pilot and feasibility studies, observational and quasi-experimental studies, formative and design studies).

\subsection{Target Conditions and Hardware Platforms}
Figure \ref{fig:disorders_devices} shows the distribution of research over time, sub-classified according to technological platforms used and mental disorders addressed. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{sections/charts/DisordersDevicesHisto.pdf}
    \caption{Joint longitudinal distribution of included papers categorized by targeted mental disorders and deployed hardware platforms.}
    \label{fig:disorders_devices}
\end{figure}

Overall, the volume of publications sharply increased since 2020-2021 in an upward trend, showing a clear peak in 2021 (8 papers) and 2025 (9 papers). This rise directly reflects the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, which had a twofold impact: a global exponential increase in psychological distress and accelerating the worldwide interest in scalable, contactless remote health technologies. The disruption of traditional, in-person clinical research during this period encouraged the push of the field toward opportunistic sensing and mobile health systems, which explains the high publication output through mid-decade.

Regarding target disorders. The focus of the research efforts is clearly toward internalizing conditions, with Depressive Disorders (51.9\%) and anxiety disorders (13.5\%) collectively accounting for 65.4\% of all studies. Those values are explained by the high global epidemiological prevalence and the strong alignment of affective symptoms with established passive digital biomarkers, such as reduced spatial mobility (GPS entropy), altered sleep-wake rhythms, and changes in device interaction. On the other hand, conditions like Neurodevelopmental Disorders (NDD, 3.8\%), bipolar disorder (1.9\%), Suicidal Ideation (SI, 1.9\%), and Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders (TSRD, 1.9\%) remain highly underrepresented. Their limited representation is largely due to significant ethical and safety constraints, particularly those related to immediate crisis response protocols for suicidal behaviour. As well as the complexity when it comes to developing models for highly episodic or clinically heterogeneous psychiatric conditions without constant clinical oversight. The distribution of Sleep-Wake (7.7\%), Substance Use (5.8\%), Somatic Symptoms (5.8\%), and Feeding/Eating Disorders (5.8\%) highlights emerging, targeted application areas in which physiological and behavioural monitoring provides immediate diagnostic value.

From a hardware perspective, mobile phones dominate the research landscape at 72.4\%, followed by tablets (15.5\%), wrist-worn wearables (6.9\%), and specialized sensors (5.2\%). Smartphones maintain their dominance in overall preference due to their ability to overcome the limitations of consumer hardware while providing a rich, multimodal array of built-in sensors ideal for continuous, unobtrusive Ecological Momentary Assessment (accelerometers, light sensors, phone-use logs). Tablets have the second-largest share primarily due to their utility in structured, clinician-guided settings or cognitive testing environments where larger displays are necessary for effective participant interaction. Interestingly, wrist-worn wearables account for a smaller share than might be expected given consumer market trends. This can be explained by the methodological challenges in academic studies such as device battery constraints, proprietary data extraction pipelines, and long-term passive sensing protocols that require participant adherence. Lastly, specialized/clinical sensors remain limited to certain applications due to their high cost, lack of interoperability, and limited feasibility for ecological, free-living monitoring.

\subsection{Captured Contextual Data:}
To minimize participant burden and interaction costs, intervention architectures increasingly rely on passive sensing to establish contextual awareness.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{sections/charts/bubbleChart.pdf}
    \label{fig:bubble_matrix}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in the Context Mapping matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:bubble_matrix}). This contextual framework is organized around three core dimensions: What contextual variables are captured, How they are technologically acquired, and What For they are used, referring to their functional clinical objective. Across 291 mapped contextual instances, including 223 context acquisition mechanisms (How) and 68 intervention objectives (What For), the overall distribution is heavily skewed toward software-level indicators. Computational Context predominates, accounting for 56.0\% of all instances (n = 163), followed by Physical Context (24.4\%, n = 71) and Social Context (19.6\%, n = 57).

Within the Computational Context domain (n=163; 123 collection mechanisms and 40 intervention targets), In-App Telemetry constitutes the largest single subcategory, accounting for 60 data points (45 mechanisms and 15 targets; 36.8\% of the domain). This is followed by Micro-Temporal patterns with 39 data points (27 mechanisms, 12 targets; 23.9\%), Task Completion metrics with 36 data points (29 mechanisms, 7 targets; 22.1\%), and Macro-Timeline behavioral rhythms with 28 data points (22 mechanisms, 6 targets; 17.2\%). The heavy concentration on in-app telemetry and micro-temporal tracking is driven by the technical accessibility of native software logging, which requires minimal user involvement and imposes no additional battery strain. These signals are directly compatible with Device-side Automation (n = 84), supporting local, low-latency processing while eliminating the need for external API calls and continuous cloud synchronization.

Regarding Physical Context (n = 71, including 53 collection mechanisms and 18 intervention targets), research effort is focused on Mobility \& Movement data, which represents 32 data points (24 mechanisms and 8 targets; 45.1\% of the domain). The remaining physical subcategories occupy smaller proportions: Semantic Context (14 data points; 9 mechanisms, 5 targets; 19.7\%), Absolute Geolocation (13 data points; 11 mechanisms, 2 targets; 18.3\%), and Ambient Environment data (12 data points; 9 mechanisms, 3 targets; 16.9\%). Mobility metrics, primarily captured via GPS/Geofencing (n = 28) and Bluetooth sensors (n = 11), are heavily favored because movement entropy and life-space mobility have been widely validated as passive biomarkers of changes in mental health. In contrast, environmental variables, such as ambient light and audio, are deployed more selectively because of increased battery consumption and privacy concerns associated with continuous acoustic and environmental monitoring.

In the Social Context domain (n=57; 47 collection mechanisms and 10 intervention targets), Communication metrics predominate with 22 data points (17 mechanisms and 5 targets; 38.6\% of the domain). This is followed by Physical Proximity sensors (16 data points; 13 mechanisms, 3 targets; 28.1\%), Peer / Community signals (13 data points; 11 mechanisms, 2 targets; 22.8\%), and Clinical / Coach Support indicators (6 data points; 6 mechanisms, 0 targets; 10.5\%). Passive communication metadata and localized proximity scanning provide unobtrusive proxies for social interaction and isolation. Alternatively, clinical and coach support metrics are the least captured passively and remain unmapped as direct "What For" target context variables. This reflects strict ethical boundaries and governance frameworks, where human-in-the-loop clinical intervention is delivered via direct contact (n=11) or crisis escalation rather than continuous passive tracking.

Synthesizing these dimensions reveals a unified architectural pipeline connecting context acquisition directly to therapeutic delivery. On the functional target side (What For), Adaptive Therapy represents the primary objective (28 data points; 41.2\%), followed by Healthcare Manager Support (25 data points; 36.8\%), Learning Algorithms (10 data points; 14.7\%), and general User Support (5 data points; 7.4\%). On the technical execution side (How), Device-side Automation (n=84; 37.7\% of mechanisms) functions as the primary delivery backbone. Taken together, these findings demonstrate a clear design paradigm: modern platforms leverage background device-side automation to transform low-friction computational and physical telemetry into real-time context models, directly driving Just-In-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs) while preserving user privacy and system efficiency.

\subsection{Inerventions mechanisms and classifcation}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{sections/charts/mechanismClassificationStacked.pdf}
    \caption{Distribution of intervention mechanisms stacked by operational classification.}
    \label{fig:intervention_mechanisms_stacked}
\end{figure}

As evident from Figure~\ref{fig:intervention_mechanisms_stacked}, there is notable variation in both overall frequency and operational classification across the six identified intervention mechanisms (comprising 49 total co-occurrences). Rather than relying on isolated delivery modes, contemporary interventions increasingly operate along a continuum of automation and human involvement. This classification describes three intervention delivery paradigms: Active interventions require direct, conscious engagement or decision-making from the user or clinician within the application; Passive interventions operate autonomously in the background without requiring user initiative; and Hybrid interventions pair automated background processes with human decision-making or collaborative workflows.

The primary mechanism across the examined corpus is Triggered delivery (n=15 co-occurrences), which relies on predefined rule-based logic to initiate interventions when specific contextual conditions are met. Triggered mechanisms display a flexible distribution across all three operational types, led by Active implementations (n=6), followed by Passive (n=5) and Hybrid (n=4) configurations. The prominence of Active triggered interventions reflects a design shift where automated rules do not merely deliver quiet background modifications, but actively prompt users to complete momentary exercises, verify context, or engage in timely behavioral reflection.

The second most common intervention mechanisms (n=9 co-occurrences each) are Algorithmic, Dashboard, and Manual interventions, each displaying multi-faceted operational profiles. Algorithmic interventions (n=9) are led by Active classifications (n=4), with substantial representation in Hybrid (n=3) and Passive (n=2) modes. This distribution highlights how machine learning and predictive models are increasingly utilized not only for silent backend parameter tuning (Passive), but also for generating real-time, user-facing recommendations that demand intentional participant action (Active) or clinical triage (Hybrid). Meanwhile, both Dashboard (n=9) and Manual (n=9) interventions exhibit a perfectly balanced split across Active (n=3), Hybrid (n=3), and Passive (n=3) classifications. For Dashboards, this reflects their evolution from passive patient-monitoring panels into active decision-support interfaces and hybrid collaborative tools. For Manual strategies, it captures workflows ranging from direct clinician outreach (Active) to automated protocol logging (Passive) and co-managed clinical interventions (Hybrid).

Occupying an intermediate tier, Escalated interventions (n=6 co-occurrences) also display an even distribution across Active (n=2), Hybrid (n=2), and Passive (n=2) classifications. Escalation architectures inherently bridge continuous automated risk detection (Passive) with structured clinical decision pipelines (Hybrid) and direct emergency interventions (Active) when safety thresholds are breached. Finally, User-driven interventions represent the least frequent mechanism (n=1 co-occurrence) and are exclusively operationalized as Active (100%). These interventions rely entirely on participant initiative to launch modules or request assistance, operating independently of background sensing or automated triggering.

Overall, these findings reveal the flexibility of digital health intervention design. Rather than relying on strictly automated or manual approaches, modern interventions use active, passive, and hybrid delivery modes according to the clinical context and the level of human oversight required. Rule-based, algorithmic, dashboard, and manual mechanisms can all be adapted to these different delivery paradigms.

\subsection{Studies Designs and Evaluation Metrics}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{sections/charts/EMSTDheatmap.pdf}
    \caption{Distribution of study designs and evaluation metrics.}
    \label{fig:metrics_designs_heatmap}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:metrics_designs_heatmap} gives an overview of the co-occurrence matrix mapping study designs against evaluation metrics($N = 42$ classified studies). Pilot \& Feasibility study designs represent the most prevalent methodology ($n = 20$; 47.6\%), followed by Experimental Randomized Controlled Trials ($n = 14$; 33.3\%), Formative \& Design studies ($n = 5$; 11.9\%), and Observational or Quasi-experimental studies ($n = 3$; 7.1\%). The strong concentration of research within pilot and feasibility frameworks reflects the evolving nature of sensing-based digital health interventions, where investigators must establish protocol viability, technical reliability, and preliminary adherence before deploying resource-intensive clinical trials.

Across evaluation metrics, cross-tabulation reveals distinct methodological priorities tailored to study maturity. Acceptability \& Feasibility measures serve as a foundational evaluation layer across nearly all designs, appearing in 100.0\% of Pilot \& Feasibility studies ($n = 20$), 100.0\% of Observational studies ($n = 3$), 85.7\% of RCTs ($n = 12$), and 80.0\% of Formative \& Design studies ($n = 4$). In parallel, Clinical Efficacy \& Effectiveness constitutes the primary outcome measure in confirmatory trial designs, captured in 100.0\% of RCTs ($n = 14$) and Observational studies ($n = 3$), as well as 80.0\% of Pilot \& Feasibility studies ($n = 16$), while appearing in only 20.0\% of Formative studies ($n = 1$). Similarly, User Engagement \& Adherence receives high emphasis in Observational studies ($n = 3$; 100.0\%), RCTs ($n = 12$; 85.7\%), and Pilot studies ($n = 15$; 75.0\%), but remains minimal during initial Formative \& Design phases ($n = 1$; 20.0\%), indicating that continuous interaction tracking becomes particularly relevant during prolonged participant engagement.

On the other hand, Usability \& UX metrics display an inverse pattern, concentrating heavily in human-centered design phases before declining in importance during late-stage evaluation. Usability criteria are predominantly applied in Formative \& Design studies ($n = 4$; 80.0\%) and Pilot \& Feasibility studies ($n = 12$; 60.0\%), whereas their inclusion drops substantially in RCTs ($n = 6$; 42.9\%) and is absent from Observational studies ($n = 0$; 0.0\%). Synthesizing these co-occurrence patterns reveals a distinct two-phase evaluation pipeline: early-stage formative and pilot research prioritizes human-computer interaction, interface usability, and operational feasibility, whereas mature RCTs and observational studies transition focus toward validating therapeutic efficacy, clinical outcomes, and long-term user retention.

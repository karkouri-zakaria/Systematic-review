% % PRISMA items 16a and 16b
\subsection{Study Selection}

Figure~\ref{fig:flow_chart} summarizes the study identification, screening, and inclusion.
The database searches retrieved 16,319 records: 5,368 from Scopus, 5,260 from Web of Science, 4,123 from PubMed, and 1,568 from APA PsycInfo. After removing 7,874 duplicates, 8,445 records remained for title, abstract, and keyword screening. We excluded 8,259 records at this stage, and we sought retrieval of the remaining 186 articles; we retrieved all and assessed them in full text. Of these, 146 were excluded because they did not meet one or more inclusion criteria: IC1, targeted disorders (n = 96); IC2, mobile mental health intervention (n = 31); IC3, external contextualization (n = 13); and IC4, type of publication (n = 4). Finally, 40 studies were included in the review.

\subsection{Target Mental Disorders (Q1)}

The eligible studies addressed nine mental health condition categories, with eight studies (20.0\%) targeting comorbid presentations, yielding 50 disorder instances across the 40 studies. Research focused predominantly on depressive disorders alone (18, 45.0\%), followed by studies addressing depressive and anxiety disorders together (6, 15.0\%). Two additional studies targeted three-condition combinations: one (2.5\%) combined depressive, anxiety, and substance-related and addictive disorders, and one (2.5\%) combined depressive, bipolar, and somatic symptom and related disorders. Across all single- and multi-condition studies, depressive disorders were targeted in 26 studies (65.0\%), and anxiety disorders in seven studies (17.5\%). The remaining conditions lagged well behind: sleep-wake disorders appeared in four studies (10.0\%), while feeding and eating disorders (3, 7.5\%), substance-related and addictive disorders (3, 7.5\%; two standalone and one comorbid), and somatic symptom and related disorders (3, 7.5\%; two standalone and one comorbid) each accounted for three studies. Neurodevelopmental disorders were addressed in two studies (5.0\%), whereas trauma- and stressor-related disorders (1, 2.5\%) and bipolar and related disorders (1, 2.5\%, in comorbidity) were the least represented. Figure~\ref{fig:disorders_devices} shows the distribution of targeted disorders over time.

% PRISMA item 20 -- Q2
\subsection{Technology Platforms (Q2)}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:disorders_devices}, four types of technology platforms were identified, with 53 platform instances across the 40 studies. As expected according to IC2, all 40 studies (100.0\%) used mobile phones, with 30 studies (75.0\%) relying exclusively on them. Overall, tablets were used in eight studies (20.0\%), wrist-worn wearables in three studies (7.5\%), and positioning sensors in two studies (5.0\%). Additional devices therefore always complemented, rather than replaced, the smartphone. Among the 10 studies (25.0\%) that combined mobile phones with secondary devices, seven (17.5\%) used a single additional device—tablets (5, 12.5\%), wrist-worn wearables (1, 2.5\%), or positioning sensors (1, 2.5\%)—while three (7.5\%) incorporated three-device combinations alongside mobile phones: two (5.0\%) combining tablets with wrist-worn wearables, and one (2.5\%) combining tablets with positioning sensors.

\subsection{External Context Types (Q3)}
\label{sec:results:q3}

%To minimize participant burden and interaction costs, intervention architectures increasingly rely on passive sensing to establish contextual awareness.

The unit of analysis in this and next sections \ref{sec:results:q4} and \ref{sec:results:q5} is a \emph{context instance}: a coded link between an external context subcategory (the labels for the marks in \textit{What?} axis Figure~\ref{fig:bubble_matrix}) and either the technique used to capture it (\textit{how?}) or the purpose for which it was used in the intervention (\textit{what for?}). Figure~\ref{fig:bubble_matrix} shows how each context instance is mapped into the three main categories (see Section~\ref{sec:methods:data-items}): \textit{what} contextual variables are captured (external context types), \textit{how} they are technologically acquired (capturing techniques), and \textit{what for} they are used, in reference to their purpose within clinical intervention. Each main category corresponds to an axis. In total, we identified 379 context instances: 205 capture techniques (\textit{how?} axis) and 174 intervention purposes (\textit{what for?} axis). To analyze the type of external context, each context instance, referred to as the \textit{what?} axis, is counted twice: once based on how it was captured (right-hand side of the figure) and once based on how it was used (left-hand side of the figure). Both sets of instances are presented separately below.

Across all three categories of external context shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bubble_matrix}, computing and temporal context accounted for the majority of instances (222 of 379; 117 capture techniques and 105 intervention purposes; 58.6\%), followed by social context (79: 43 and 36; 20.8\%) and physical context (78: 45 and 33; 20.6\%).

Within the computing and temporal context (222 instances), in-app telemetry was the largest subcategory (82 instances; 36.9\%; 44 capture techniques and 38 intervention purposes), followed by task completion metrics (53; 23.9\%; 28 and 25), micro-temporal patterns (45; 20.3\%; 23 and 22), and macro-timeline patterns (42; 18.9\%; 22 and 20).

Regarding physical context (78 instances), research effort focused largely on the mobility and movement subcategory (36 instances; 46.2\%; 20 capture techniques and 16 intervention purposes), followed by semantic context (17; 21.8\%; 9 and 8), ambient environment (15; 19.2\%; 9 and 6), and absolute geolocation (10; 12.8\%; 7 and 3).

Within the social context (79 instances), communication metrics were the largest subcategory (30 instances; 38.0\%; 17 capture techniques and 13 intervention purposes), followed by peer/community signals (20; 25.3\%; 11 and 9), physical proximity (16; 20.3\%; 9 and 7), and clinical/coach support indicators (13; 16.5\%; 6 and 7).

\subsection{Use/Purpose of External Context in Interventions (Q5)}
\label{sec:results:q5}

Of the 174 instances between context and intervention purpose (\textit{What For?} axis in Figure~\ref{fig:bubble_matrix}), user support (e.g., psychoeducation, self-management tools) was the most frequent purpose (99 context instances; 56.9\%), followed by adaptive therapy (40; 23.0\%), healthcare provider support (29; 16.7\%), and learning algorithms (6; 3.4\%). %On the technical execution side, Device-side Automation (n = 78; 38.0\% of mechanisms) functions as the primary delivery backbone.
Most user-support context instances came from computing and temporal context; in-app telemetry alone was linked to user support 26 times. Context was thus used mainly to inform users directly, such as informative messages, custom feedback, and notifications, and rarely to train predictive or personalization models.

\subsection{Context Delivery Mechanisms (Q6)}
\label{sec:results:q6}
% Q5, second part: "which specific mechanisms are employed in intervention delivery?
As regards delivery mechanisms and paradigms, we identified six delivery mechanisms represented by 117 coded mechanism-paradigm co-occurrences. Each co-occurrence was classified into one of three delivery paradigms: active, requiring direct, conscious engagement or decision-making from the user or clinician; passive, operating autonomously in the background; and hybrid, pairing automated processes with human decision-making or collaborative workflows.

Figure~\ref{fig:intervention_mechanisms_stacked} shows the distribution across the three intervention delivery paradigms. Active delivery predominated (69 co-occurrences; 59.0\%), followed by hybrid (33; 28.2\%) and passive delivery (15; 12.8\%). Active delivery was the most frequent paradigm for every mechanism except dashboards and escalated delivery mechanisms, which belonged predominantly to the hybrid paradigm.

User-driven delivery, in which participants themselves launch modules or request support, was by far the most frequent mechanism (45 co-occurrences; 38.5\%: 34 active, 7 hybrid, 4 passive). It was followed by triggered delivery, in which predefined rules start the intervention when specific contextual conditions are met (24; 20.5\%: 14 active, 6 hybrid, 4 passive), and manual delivery (21; 17.9\%: 11 active, 7 hybrid, 3 passive). Dashboard-based delivery (14; 12.0\%: 5 active, 7 hybrid, 2 passive), algorithmic delivery (9; 7.7\%: 4 active, 4 hybrid, 1 passive), and escalated delivery mechanisms (4; 3.4\%: 1 active, 2 hybrid, 1 passive) were less common configurations.

\subsection{Evaluation Designs and Dimensions (Q7)}

Figure~\ref{fig:metrics_designs_heatmap} gives an overview of the co-occurrence matrix mapping study designs against evaluation metrics ($N = 40$ classified studies). Pilot and feasibility studies were the most common design (18 studies; 45.0\%), followed by randomized controlled trials (RCTs; 14; 35.0\%), formative and design studies (5; 12.5\%), and observational or quasi-experimental studies (3; 7.5\%).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{sections/charts/EMSTDheatmap.pdf}
    \caption{Number of studies by study design and evaluation dimension ($N = 40$).}

Across all 40 studies, acceptability and feasibility were assessed in 37 (92.5\%), clinical efficacy and effectiveness in 33 (82.5\%), user engagement and adherence in 29 (72.5\%), and usability and user experience in 22 (55.0\%). Figure~\ref{fig:metrics_designs_heatmap} shows how these dimensions vary by design.
